about: Individual · Could Consciousness be Relational?
passage-label: Passage · Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §192-193
passage: “In this recognition the unessential consciousness [of the servant] is for the master the object which constitutes the truth of his certainty of himself. But it is clear that this object does not correspond to its concept, but rather that the object in which the master has achieved his mastery has in reality turned out to be something quite different from an independent consciousness. What now really confronts him is not an independent consciousness, but a dependent one. He is, therefore, not certain of being-for-self as the truth of himself. In these terms, the truth of the independent consciousness lies in fact within the servile consciousness. At first, this consciousness admittedly appears outside itself and not as the truth of self-consciousness. But just as mastery showed that its essential nature proves the reverse of what it wants to be, so too servitude will ultimately turn into the opposite of what it immediately is; as a consciousness forced back into itself, it will withdraw into itself and transform into a truly independent consciousness” (Hegel, 192-193).

In its most common connotations, the paired terms master and servant explore a relationship in which the servant is inferior to the master. This dynamic is widely explored and ever-present throughout history in political, economic, and social contexts. Often, such relationships appear straightforward: the master controls their independence and holds power over the servant who exists in a state of dependence. Hegel challenges this assumption in his master-slave dialectic, however. Hegel introduces the idea that while the master is known to have physical control over the servant, the control he possesses is not fully autonomous.

This reversal of power introduces a far deeper philosophical concern. The issue at hand is not merely one of the relationships between master and servant, but of the relationship between consciousnesses. Hegel presents the concept that consciousness cannot fully understand itself in a state of isolation in saying “the truth of the independent consciousness lies in fact within the servile consciousness” (193). Instead, self-consciousness could require the presence of another consciousness.

Initially, this claim seems to contradict itself. The servant is in a subordinate position while the master appears self-determining. Hegel, however, argues that this apparent independence is unstable. Although the master exercises physical control, the master’s self-certainty is dependent on recognition from the servant. The servant, in turn, functions as the object that the master grounds his own status and identity.

This idea is central to Hegel’s concept of self-consciousness. Instead of operating as its own entity, consciousness appears to require a form of recognition from another consciousness in order to achieve knowledge of itself. Thus, the value of the servant to the master is not solely labor, but the role the servant’s consciousness plays in the development of the master’s self-consciousness. Hegel’s claim of “the truth of the independent consciousness lies in fact within the servile consciousness” (193) can be understood as an attempt to demonstrate that self-consciousness may only emerge through interaction instead of isolation. The servant develops the consciousness upon which the master depends through labor and the shaping of external objects. By working upon the world under the discipline of fear and dependence, the servant gains a greater awareness of both self and reality. In contrast, the master's relationship with the external world remains indirect, as it is mediated through the labor of the servant.

If consciousness emerges through interaction for the master, a similar process occurs for the consciousness of the servant, though not in precisely the same way. While the master gains self-consciousness primarily through recognition from another consciousness, the servant develops self-consciousness through both recognition and direct interaction with the external world. The servant originates from a different standpoint in this relationship, one that puts them as a dependent entity. In saying “But just as mastery showed that its essential nature proves the reverse of what it wants to be, so too servitude will ultimately turn into the opposite of what it immediately is” (193), Hegel further develops this reversal. Provided that consciousness depends on another for self-consciousness, the servant develops a self-consciousness that is less reliant on recognition than the master’s through reflection, labor, and living through dependence. Because the servant actively shapes the external world rather than merely receiving recognition from another consciousness, the servant develops a more substantial understanding of self.

If the servant becomes independent through a relationship of dependence, then consciousness may not become itself through isolation but through relations with others. Beginning from a position of dependence forces the servant to confront the limits of self-consciousness in a way the master never does. The servant must develop a sense of self through reflection on this condition. As Hegel says, “as a consciousness forced back into itself, it will withdraw into itself and transform into a truly independent consciousness” (193). The servant’s prior dependence becomes the basis for a deeper self-consciousness. In contrast, the master's established sense of independence prevents such reflection. This leaves the master’s self-certainty dependent upon recognition from another consciousness.

While both consciousnesses require recognition, the servant’s experience of dependence allows the servant to develop beyond that initial dependence. Unlike the master, who remains dependent upon recognition from the servant, the servant gains self-consciousness through direct engagement with the external world. This raises the possibility that the servant attains a more genuine form of self-consciousness than the master despite beginning from a position of subordination. Hegel’s master-slave dialectic portrays consciousness as a force that develops through recognition and appears to shape human relations. If consciousness emerges from interaction instead of isolation, then consciousness may do more than simply accompany human experiences. At the same time, Hegel leaves unresolved whether these transformations are caused by consciousness itself or by the social interactions in which self-consciousness develops. The dialectic thus suggests that consciousness matters to human development while still imposing the question of how, or whether, consciousness itself exerts causation.

about: Individual · Relationships in Consciousness: The Evolution

In Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit, he investigates the evolution of self-consciousness through a sequence of experiences rather than a single idea. Each experience reveals the limitations of the stage that preceded it, allowing consciousness to develop a deeper understanding of itself. This development is further shaped through relationships with others. The master-slave relationship provides a strong example of this process, as it demonstrates the relational aspect of consciousness. Hegel presents consciousness as a journey of relational introspection, while showing that this development occurs through struggle and transformation.

This narrative begins with Hegel’s concept of “otherness,” which refers to what consciousness recognizes as distinct from itself. Consciousness begins to understand itself by determining what is self and what is not self. Recognizing another sentient being as an other is therefore key to developing a sense of self. Hegel uses this concept to demonstrate that consciousness does not become self-aware through enlightenment alone; instead, it emerges through interaction with what exists beyond itself. Through these encounters, consciousness comes to understand what the self is by recognizing what exists outside of it.

How, then, does consciousness shift from simply recognizing an other to becoming self-conscious? Hegel develops this question through the idea of desire. Consciousness desires objects because they appear separate from itself and capable of satisfying its needs. However, experience teaches consciousness that these objects possess their own independence. The world is not simply a product of consciousness’s desires. This realization demonstrates that reality exists beyond consciousness’s immediate perception and allows consciousness to begin recognizing independence in both objects and other beings.

Once this differentiation is established, another problem emerges. If self-consciousness requires recognition from another consciousness, what happens when two consciousnesses encounter one another? Hegel presents this encounter as a struggle for recognition. Each consciousness desires acknowledgment of its independence and seeks confirmation of itself, creating what Hegel describes as a “double-edged” relationship in which each consciousness attempts to supersede the other while returning to itself through the struggle (181). This conflict produces the master-slave relationship, a pivotal stage in the development of consciousness.

After this struggle, the master appears to have achieved victory. The slave submits to dependence while the master secures independence and recognition. However, Hegel reveals a contradiction: the master’s recognition comes from a dependent consciousness rather than an equal one. Because of this dependence, the recognition fails to provide the genuine confirmation the master seeks. The master’s apparent victory therefore becomes another limitation that consciousness must overcome.

The slave’s path is initially defined by fear and dependence. However, the slave develops through labor and engagement with the material world. By transforming objects through work, the slave encounters the independence of reality while also recognizing an ability to shape it. Labor therefore becomes a means through which consciousness develops. The slave’s dependency, which initially appeared to be a weakness, ultimately becomes the condition that allows for greater transformation and self-understanding.

The importance of the master-slave relationship extends beyond its outcome. If Hegel’s only purpose were to establish the necessity of mutual recognition, the relationship would not need to develop through such a complex sequence of reversals. Instead, Hegel presents consciousness as developing through experiences that reveal the limitations of previous stages. The master’s initial victory proves contradictory, while the slave’s defeat becomes the source of growth. Through these reversals, consciousness learns from its failures and reaches understandings that were impossible at earlier stages.

Viewed this way, the master-slave relationship is more than a relationship of domination and dependence; it is a crucial part of the narrative emergence of consciousness. Each stage develops from the one before it, creating a progression through which consciousness comes to understand itself. Hegel shows that self-consciousness cannot appear all at once as a finished achievement. It develops through struggle, recognition, and transformation, with each experience revealing a deeper truth about the self. By presenting consciousness as an evolving story rather than a static concept, Hegel shows that genuine self-knowledge arises through the process of becoming rather than simply being.

about: Individual · Narration within Hegel

Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit presents consciousness as something that comes to understand itself through a series of struggles. These struggles, involving dominance, recognition by an other, and transformation of itself, allow one to gain a truly deep understanding of self. This necessity is the mark of narration: consciousness can reach self-understanding only through a sequence of successive states with a purposive design. One might object, however, that such an evolution need not occur under the presumption of narration, since each stage follows from the contradiction in the one before it, and necessity of that kind looks logical rather than narrative.

Hegel’s process may help us answer this objection. The sequence he describes follows a process of supersession. Consciousness struggles, finds a limitation in its present state, and moves toward a fuller understanding of self. Each stage appears driven by the contradiction in the one before it. The struggle for recognition, for example, grows from a desire for dominance within consciousness itself. If this governs every transition, Hegel may be forming a logical system instead of telling a story of consciousness. In this way, progression would seem to follow a rigid logical structure.

The objection grows stronger when we investigate where Hegel locates this development. He does not use introspection as the primary route to self-consciousness. Instead, consciousness develops through its encounter with an other, and the two consciousnesses “recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another” (184). This excerpt displays how each self depends on the other for what it is. What begins as a struggle for dominance becomes a struggle for recognition. This results in the master-servant relation according to a structure that neither party can control. If so, progression seems governed again by a principle rather than by any one person’s decisions. Can narration capture what Hegel is doing at all?

Reducing Hegel’s account to only a logical process ignores the way he presents consciousness as evolving through successive transformations. Narration does not require a character with dialogue; rather, it aligns states into a meaningful sequence that holds purpose and direction. The master-servant relation shows each of these elements. The struggle for recognition ends with one consciousness as master and the other as servant. The failures built into each position coordinate it with what follows. The importance of these moments is not merely their logical connection but the way they produce transformation over time.

The master’s position after his victory in the struggle is the first failure. It is a failure because recognition comes from someone he sees as a thing rather than as an equal, and the recognition is therefore empty. His self-certainty holds little truth, as he is certain of self only through a consciousness whose acknowledgment he has already discounted (191). The servant’s position is where the contradiction is resolved. Through interaction with the world the master lacks, the servant cancels “his dependence on and attachment to natural existence” (194). While he began as the dependent one, labor lets him recognize his own impact on the world, and he gains a deeper consciousness than the master. This outcome results from the earlier states: the struggle, the failure of recognition, and the work itself. Without these aspects, the transformation of the servant would not make sense.

Taken together, the two positions show a design with a purpose. The master fails because his recognition is one-sided, while the servant develops through the labor the master cannot perform. This movement points toward mutual recognition, the form of self-understanding the sequence works toward. That neither party controls the structure does not remove the narration, since narration requires coordination and purpose, not individual choice. The necessity the objection points to is therefore not a rival to narration. It is the reason consciousness’s development must take narrative form. Consciousness cannot reach self-understanding immediately; it becomes capable of understanding itself and others only by passing through successive states of failure and transformation.

Hegel’s account thus suggests that necessity and narration are not opposing explanations of consciousness. The contradictions that constrain consciousness also create the conditions for its development. Each stage inherits the limitations of the one before it while making possible a transformation that could not have occurred in isolation. Narration gives this necessary movement its intelligible form: consciousness does not simply arrive at self-understanding, but becomes capable of it through the process that leads there.

about: Individual · Hegel and Scarry on narration

How could two authors separated by almost 200 years hold such similar assumptions about consciousness, despite the ever-changing human race? The question points to enduring features of human experience that persist through historical change. Both Hegel and Scarry understand consciousness through narration. For each, consciousness is not an immediate state one is, say, born with, but something that becomes sensible through a process of transformation. In Phenomenology of Spirit, Hegel presents consciousness as successive stages that evolve through a struggle for recognition between two parties. In The Body in Pain, Scarry argues that pain and creation turn subjective experience into something external and communicable to others. While the authors focus on different experiences of the external world, both suggest that consciousness can be understood only through a sequence of changes that give meaning to otherwise solitary concepts or objects. Their shared emphasis on transformation points to narration as a fundamental quality of consciousness.

Still, the two use narration differently, which raises a further question: what is narration even for? For Hegel, narration is developmental. Consciousness begins to understand itself more deeply by resolving contradictions, and each resolved contradiction brings it closer to self-knowledge because its failures create opportunities for further growth. The servant depicts this evolution most clearly. Through labor, he reaches a deeper awareness of self through his interaction with the world, and Hegel explains that “the bondsman becomes conscious of what he truly is” (196). Scarry, on the other hand, is less concerned with progress through stages than with expression. The central problem of pain is that it resists language and threatens to lock the sufferer into a purely internal experience. Narration becomes necessary not because it advances consciousness through stages, but because it lets consciousness externalize itself through acts of creation and thus shared objects. Hegel’s narrative structure explains how consciousness develops; Scarry’s explains how it becomes communicable.

Scarry maintains that physical pain has no content to refer to and actively destroys language, which makes acts of creation essential for expression. She describes creation as “the making of what is originally interior and private into something exterior and sharable” (284), and a made object as “a projection of the human body” (281). Hegel also makes consciousness depend on what lies outside it, but he locates that dependence in social recognition: “Self-consciousness achieves its satisfaction only in another self-consciousness” (175). However, the servant complicates this acknowledgment. His labor is a form of creation, and through it he finds himself in the object he has transformed. This is where Hegel and Scarry come closest: the servant’s self-knowledge depends on material creation as well as recognition. The two accounts are therefore less opposed than they first appear. Hegel emphasizes interpersonal recognition and Scarry objectification, but the servant’s work shows both can occur together.

This reading invites an objection. If each of Hegel’s stages follows from the last with logical necessity, the movement looks like deduction rather than story, and the course definition of narration, a “coordination of successive states, often over extended stretches of time, into a sequence with a design that gives purpose,” seems to demand more than necessity. The objection is right that the stages are tightly connected, but wrong that they are therefore not narrative. Consciousness lives each stage as an experience and must work through its failure, as the servant does. Scarry sharpens the point: if narration makes consciousness communicable, then Hegel’s sequence is the form in which consciousness becomes visible to itself, a design that gives purpose. I hold this as probable rather than certain, since a reader who sees the stages as purely logical could still resist it.

Beneath these differences lies a common insight: consciousness cannot fully understand itself in isolation. Hegel shows self-consciousness emerging through recognition and social development, while Scarry shows consciousness projecting itself outward to overcome the limits of private experience. I began by assuming narration meant ordered development toward a goal, which is how Hegel’s stages first looked to me. Reading Scarry changed that assumption. Narration is more than a sequence of events; it is the process by which consciousness becomes visible both to itself and to others. This extends the definition developed in earlier builds: narration does not merely organize successive stages but transforms subjective experience into meaningful knowledge. Consciousness does not simply follow a narrative structure. It depends on narration to make itself intelligible at all.
