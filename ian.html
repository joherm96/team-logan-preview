    Passage · Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §195“Although the fear of the master is the beginning of wisdom, consciousness is not therein aware of being-for-self.Through work, however, the servant becomes conscious of what he truly is. [... The master’s] desire has reserved to itself the pure negating of the object and thereby an unalloyed feeling of self. But that is the reason why this satisfaction is itself only a fleeting one, for it lacks objectivity and permanence. Work, on the other hand, requires desire held in check, fleetingness staved off; in other words,work forms and shapes the thing. [...] This negative middle term or formative activity is at the same time the individuality or pure being-for-self of consciousness which now, in the work outside of it, acquires an element of permanence. It is in this way, therefore, that consciousness, as a worker, comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independent being” (195).

    In a previous passage within his account of self-consciousness, Hegel rests his case upon the rather dramatic claim that “A self-consciousness exists for a self-consciousness” (177), that is, that a mind can not know itself without knowing something of someone else’s mind as well. Yet, when Hegel introduces self-consciousness and its independence later on in the 195th section, the one I selected, he makes a somewhat contradictory statement regarding the servant and his achieving this independent status with no other consciousness present. Hegel’s quote regarding the servant’s means of gaining self-consciousness reads “consciousness, as a worker, comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independent being” (195). This second, contradictory, statement raises an important question; if selfhood requires recognition by another, independent, entity, how is it possible for the servant to gain consciousness through his work which grants him no consciousness?

    How can the servant obtain self-consciousness while doing his work only on inanimate objects? That is the question Hegel touches on in the 195th section as well as later sections in his work. Hegel describes that “Work … requires desire held in check, fleetingness staved off” (195). In this, Hegel shows that through physical work, whether it includes another consciousness or not, a human has life experiences such as satisfaction. While this doesn’t answer the overarching question of whether or not selfhood requires recognition by another party, it does begin to describe the benefits of working for oneself.

    In the middle of the passage lies another question posed by Hegel. Why does work succeed while desire fails to create a person’s feeling of self? The master achieves a satisfaction that is “fleeting” whereas the servant’s work creates what Hegel calls the “negative middle” (195) or, in other words, the process that stands between the servant and his ascension to self-consciousness. Hegel describes the master’s work as “fleeting,” as he does no real work on the object and therefore can take no credit for anything. In his claim regarding the “negative middle,” Hegel explains that because the servant can look back at his work and see the independent object he has created or done work upon, he will begin to understand and realize his own agency.

    Hegel’s argument about the agency of the servant is evident throughout the entire passage and work as a whole. With this, it must be noted that he goes so far as to make the claim that the negative middle term “is … the individuality … of consciousness” (195). So not only is Hegel making the claim that the servant’s object reflects themself, but he also makes the distinction that the process of working is actually the servant’s self being reflected into object form. Is Hegel arguing that a person is what they do, or that a person does what they are? One could argue that because a person, from an outsider’s perspective, is only what they have experienced then they are just a sum of their total experiences. Others, however, could also argue the contrary and state that a lot of what makes a person is internal, and not every aspect of every being must or even can be measurable or observable by others.

    Throughout his 195th passage, Hegel touches on two main aspects of consciousness and what makes a human being self-conscious. His first topic touched upon was the idea that through physical work, whether that work is being done on a live or on an inanimate object, a servant can still feel the satisfaction of doing something by oneself and can still learn and enhance their own consciousness. The second topic made evident by Hegel in this section is that a master’s relation to their servant’s object is secondary, so he, the master that is, can never see himself or his work amount to anything. In this, his “satisfaction” at the work being done is more lived through his servant and not his own experiences. The relationship between the servant and his object of work is a building block of consciousness, whereas the relationship between the servant and the master is one that can be seen as a causation of fleeting satisfaction.

    Notable Comments Received

    Some notes that were instrumental in my editing and development of this essay were elaborating on the word “fleeting” in the fourth paragraph, retouching on how relationships built consciousness in the conclusion, and restating my conclusion to prove the point that the master’s relationship to the object having work done upon it was secondary, and because of that his "satisfaction" is more lived through his servant rather than his own experience. The first comment I got was the comment that allowed me to dive deeper into Hegel’s word choice and why he chose to use such diction, this led me to my first claim about the “negative middle.” The second and third comments were instrumental in the development of my overall arguments regarding the secondary nature of the master to the object and how that affected his emotions and self-being.

    Individual · Hegel on deliberation

    In Hegel’s The Phenomenology of Spirit, a large importance is placed on what we choose not to do, that is, thinking about what isn’t the case rather than what is.

    Read Build 3

    In Hegel’s The Phenomenology of Spirit, a large importance is placed on what we choose not to do, that is, thinking about what isn’t the case rather than what is. It is imperative that this is noted, seeing as the master-servant dialectic and other points being made in the text are directly related to this idea of deliberation. The definition of deliberation that will be utilized in this writing is one that explains the contemplation of multiple scenarios and how a human can react to the same situation in a plethora of different ways.

    In his writing of the master-servant dialectic, Hegel makes clear that it is ultimately this idea of deliberation that allows the servant to feel self-conscious and satisfied. Hegel writes “but the master, who has interposed the servant between the thing and himself, takes to himself only the dependent aspect of the thing and has the pure enjoyment of it. The aspect of its independence he leaves to the servant, who works on the thing” (190). In this, the master simply takes what has been done by the servant and consumes the product, whereas the servant is the entity deliberating and putting their time and energy into this creation. Hegel figures the master’s desire to be “fleeting” and lacking “objectivity and permanence” (195).

    So, with this deliberation in mind, why does Hegel still make the claim that a “self-consciousness exists for a self-consciousness” (177)? If what Hegel said about deliberation is true, and that self-consciousness comes from analyzing what not to do in a situation, then why is it still imperative for a self-consciousness to see itself in someone else’s consciousness? Hegel seeks to address this disparity by claiming that these two ideas can work in tandem with each other. In Hegel’s argument regarding deliberation being a step in the path to self-consciousness and understanding, he makes the distinction that the other’s self-consciousness is the thing that holds one’s own self-consciousness in check throughout this process. Hegel writes “Now, this movement of self-consciousness in relation to another self-consciousness has in this way been represented as the action of one self-consciousness, but this action of the one has itself the double significance of being both its own action and the action of the other as well” (182). In other words, when a self-consciousness acts independently, it does only what it pleases, but when another self-consciousness enters the picture, now any actions have implications on an other. This phenomena is the cause for Hegel’s belief of the regulation powers of another Self-consciousness.

    Through work, the servant “becomes for himself” (196). At first, the servant is bound by the master’s controls. Through work, however, the servant is able to think and be for himself; this, in and of itself, is part of the path the servant takes to self-consciousness. Now, with no checks on his state of mind, the servant can regulate his thought, and with that, consciousness. This ties back to deliberation due to the fact that deliberation needs the individual to exist on their own. At first, the servant merely carries out the doing of the servant, so deliberation is not possible. However, as the servant understands and begins to work for himself, he is now able to think and act independently.

    Next, Hegel touches on stoicism and skepticism. In the 198th section, Hegel defines stoicism as simply the freedom a person has to think. This, he outlines, is the first step of deliberation. Skepticism, as Hegel explains it in the 206th section, is the negating of the “other side of determinate existence,” or, in other words, using process of elimination to officially decide on an action. This is the second aspect of deliberation; the process of contemplating multiple scenarios and deciding on a course of action after this thought process has taken place.

    Deliberation, or the ability to eliminate ideas until left with the best option, is not something that anybody can obtain. Rather, it is something that comes with self-determination and development. True deliberation entails that one owns their actions. Through stoicism, a person can think, and through skepticism, that same person can deliberate and analyze what is best for them. This freedom to think, paired with Hegel’s statement that the other is there as a check on one’s actions, combined efforts to create the central argument of the reading; we learn to deliberate through the work we complete for others, but once that work has been finished, we can then think and act for ourselves.

    Individual · Qualifying the deliberation claim

    In the previous build, I focused on the idea of deliberation, or the contemplation of multiple scenarios and how a human can react to the same situation in a plethora of different ways.

    Read Build 4

    In the previous build, I focused on the idea of deliberation, or the contemplation of multiple scenarios and how a human can react to the same situation in a plethora of different ways. After being introduced to deliberation, I immediately formed some clear connections between the actions within the master-servant dialectic and this idea of contemplation and reaction possibilities. Contrary to my initial inclination of Hegel’s arguments, Hegel tends to provide evidence supporting both sides to many of his central arguments, suggesting deliberation isn’t the only path to self-consciousness.

    While in build three I claimed that deliberation was ultimately the reasoning behind the servant’s push towards self-consciousness, I now understand it’s imperative to note the other things Hegel credits for said transformation as well. According to Hegel, “the two moments of fear and service as such, as well as that of formative activity, are necessary” (196) to activate this transformative process. In other words, for the servant to gain self-consciousness, he must fear and provide service for the master. In this, Hegel makes no note of deliberation or any other type of decision making in the process of achieving self-consciousness, suggesting this may be another possible scenario that could lead to the same outcome. Additionally, Hegel’s explanation of self-consciousness being won through risk of one’s life, not a deliberation of one’s options, provides more evidence against the claim that deliberation is ultimately the reasoning behind the servant’s push towards self-consciousness. In the 187th section, Hegel writes that “it is solely by staking one’s life that freedom is won.” According to Hegel, one must prove that they are independent; it’s not a title one is born with. Given the previous information, it’s essential to note that all of these ideas work in tandem rather than against each other. Hegel makes numerous claims regarding how a person earns the right to gain self-consciousness, but that’s not to say that one is more or less correct or important than the others.

    Another claim I made in build three that I now see needs qualification is that stoicism and skepticism are the first and second steps to deliberation. In his The Phenomenology of Spirit, Hegel writes “This freedom of self-consciousness when it appeared as a conscious manifestation in the history of Spirit has, as we know, been called Stoicism” (198). In this, Hegel describes stoicism as a kind of freedom of the mind rather than as a step towards the ability to make decisions and think for oneself. While some could argue that these two ideas work harmoniously, it must be noted that Hegel does make this distinction between physical freedom and what is the freedom to deliberate. It may seem as though skepticism and deliberation get at the same freedom of actions, but deliberation results in choice, whereas skepticism results in a being that is contradictory and one Hegel describes as having an “Unhappy consciousness” (206). Some may argue that the duality of the being is, in and of itself, indicative of that being’s inner skeptic, but Hegel’s earlier claim that deliberation requires a unified self proves this idea wrong. In other words, skepticism splits a being into two, conflicting sides, creating what Hegel describes as the “Unhappy Consciousness.” Because deliberation requires a unified self, one who has become a skeptic can no longer deliberate.

    Deliberation is neither the reasoning behind the servant’s push to self-consciousness nor is it something stoicism and skepticism can work together to build. Rather than relying on deliberation to push himself towards self-consciousness, the servant relies not only on fear and service, but also on risking his life, leaving the impression that “freedom is won” (187). Stoicism removes the self from any action at all, and skepticism divides a consciousness in two; both of which push the self away from deliberation rather than towards it.

    This analysis of self-consciousness has led me to the conclusion that no single thing produces self-consciousness. Rather, it happens through a multitude of things such as struggle, relationships, self-reflection, and analysation.

  2. Reverie 02

    Continuity of Identity

    I believe that some traits need to stay the same within in order for a person to be considered as the same person. While your body features might not look or even function the same as you get older, some aspects of your memory and even your habits will stay with you for your entire life. I believe that a person can not be themselves without at least some aspects of their memory or previous life experiences.

  3. Reverie 03

    Repetitive Behavior That Changed Me

    In certain classes in high school, memorization and remembering terms was a large part of what was required for the course work. For me, the repetitive behavior of looking at terms, understanding what they meant, and maybe even writing a sentence involving that term was a large part of what it meant for me to understand and remember small pieces of information. Not only was this repetition used to allow me to understand what I needed to remember, but it also taught me that the brain can actually do and remember an incredible amount of information, you just need to expose yourself to that information enough times first.

  4. Reverie 04

    Detachment vs. Discipline

    I feel as though in terms of models for achieving consciousness, discipline is a more applicable model than detachment is. Detachment is an idea that represents a set outcome; nothing you do matters and you will not achieve anything through your actions. Discipline is more applicable to me because repetition can build autonomy, where detachment does not.

  5. Reverie 05

    Scarry on Consciousness

    There are no “feeling” emotions, you can only feel like you are feeling something. Also, the first step to empathy is through the projection of our own emotions. Children start their lives practicing projection on inanimate objects like dolls and toys. Scarry views this practice as preparation for when the child grows up and has to interact with other beings.

  6. Reverie 06

    Wittgenstein on Consciousness

    Wittgenstein would likely describe consciousness as a combination of many different thoughts and ideas always shifting and changing. While this may be true, I also believe that maybe he wouldn’t be the type of person to have a definite definition of consciousness. His writing describes how overlapping ideas make up larger, common, categories, which is an idea that could be transferred over to the idea of consciousness.

  7. Reverie 07

    Wittgenstein on Freedom

    Wittgenstein would likely describe freedom as an idea that is made up and can be seen as the sum of many different factors. Can a person speak for themselves? Can a person feel and act based solely on what they want? These are just a few of the factors that could go into Wittgenstein’s definition of freedom.

  8. Reverie 08

    Wittgenstein on the Self

    Wittgenstein would likely describe “self” as something that is made up of many different aspects of our past and present personalities. He would point out that our bodies and ideas have changed over time, and what remains are remnants of older times and present aspects of ourselves. Wittgenstein would urge us to look at both the past, present, and how they overlap in order to really understand what our “self” was.

Because the hosts don’t have free will and can’t act for themselves, they don’t have the deliberative ability that self-conscious people do. While I can’t pick a host who shows deliberation, I can show an example of a host showing the opposite trait. At the very end of the first season, Maeve shows the opposite of deliberation when she believes she used freedom in the way she acted out and escaped the park. However, we later learn that despite her rebellious nature, it was all still part of her code and nothing she ever did was via free will.
