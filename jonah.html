    Build 1

    Individual · Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §187

    In section 187, Hegel proposes that the only stake that can prove one's worth as a self-consciousness is one's own life.

    Read Build 1
    Passage · Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §187Insofar as it is what is done by the other, each thus aims at the death of the other. But in doing so, what is done by way of oneself is also involved, for the former involves putting one's own life on the line. Thus the relation of both self-consciousnesses means that only through a life-and-death struggle can each prove its worth to itself, and that both prove their worth to each other. They must engage in this struggle, for each must elevate the self-certainty of existing for themselves to truth, both in the case of the other and in their own case. And it is solely by staking one's life that freedom is won, namely, that as a result the essential nature of self-consciousness is proven to be not just being, not in the immediate way self-consciousness emerges, not in being absorbed within the expanse of life, but rather, that there is nothing on hand in itself which could not be a vanishing moment for it, that is, that self-consciousness is only pure being-for-itself. The individual who has not risked their life may admittedly be recognized as a person, but they have not attained the truth of being recognized as an independent self-consciousness.

    In section 187, Hegel proposes that the only stake that can prove one's worth as a self-consciousness is one's own life. Nothing else compares to it: money, possessions, reputation, even the people one loves all fall short. This raises the obvious question: why should life be the only stake that counts? Hegel's answer is that risking one's life means overcoming the survival instinct. Biology tells every living creature to stay alive at any cost, and a being that simply obeys that instinct is "absorbed within the expanse of life" (187). The only way to prove that one is more than a living body is to risk the very thing biology demands one protect. By showing that survival is not what one values most, one moves beyond being merely a person and is recognized as an independent self-consciousness. The servant is the one who refuses this stake: faced with death, the servant chooses to live. Hegel's verdict is that the servant may be recognized as a person, but not as an independent self-consciousness. Yet is this refusal really a choice?

    At first, Hegel's language suggests that the servant's surrender is not a free choice at all. In section 189 he describes the servant as a consciousness whose "essential nature is simply to live, or to be, for another" (189). On this reading, it is biology telling the servant to surrender, not a self-consciousness choosing to defy that biology. I believe, however, that the servant's surrender is a genuine choice. A choice made under threat of death looks like coercion, since the only alternative is to die. But by Hegel's own logic, having death as one option does not disqualify a choice from being free. In fact, it is the only kind of choice that truly tests freedom. Hegel insists that the only way to win freedom is by staking one's life, and the servant has plainly failed to meet this condition. Yet later in the chapter, Hegel writes that servitude will "transform into a truly independent consciousness" (193). This apparent contradiction leads to the next question: how can the one who refused the stake be the one who ends up free?

    The servant does not win freedom in a single moment. Rather, the servant builds and earns it over time. In section 196, Hegel names three things this requires: fear, service, and work. Fear is the servant's encounter with death, "the absolute Master" (194). Service is "the discipline of service and obedience" (196), and work is the activity that "forms and shapes the thing" (195). The master, by contrast, did stake their life, yet by these same measures never becomes truly free. The master could only be free if recognized as free by an equal, and this is never possible. Either the master is recognized by someone beneath them, whose recognition counts for nothing, or the master meets another master and they fight to the death to decide who is free. One of them must surrender and becomes a servant, and the cycle begins again. While the master believes their consciousness can only be confirmed through another's recognition, the servant finds that confirmation in their work: looking at something they made, the servant "comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence" (195). This suggests an answer to the question I began with. The servant never stakes their life, but day after day the servant gives up their own choices, their desires, and their time, submitting their will to the work. In exchange, the servant gains a freedom that is concrete rather than abstract, visible in the things they shape. Perhaps life is not the only stake that counts. Perhaps a life can also be staked slowly, through service, rather than all at once.

    I have not settled whether the stake has to be life at all. Part of what gives death its weight is that it cannot be undone, yet in Westworld, Maeve provokes her own death knowing she will wake up in the lab (1.6). By that standard her death stakes nothing, and still it moves her toward freedom. Is she staking her life, or something slower, the way the servant does? Nor have I settled whether anyone in the dialectic truly chooses. If the servant's surrender was only biology after all, then the master's refusal to surrender may be biology as well. In that case, was anyone choosing anything, or was the whole struggle scripted in advance, like a host's loop?

    Build 3

    Individual · Hegel and Fanon on deliberation

    Is staking one's life enough to make one's self truly conscious?

    Read Build 3

    Is staking one's life enough to make one's self truly conscious? At first, I took Hegel to mean that true consciousness requires risking the one thing you can't come back from, your life. I now think that is too simple. When the two self-consciousnesses first meet, each sees only itself in the other and never recognizes the other as a self. Consciousness also requires recognition from both sides, and that is what the first meeting fails to produce. So the servant turns to his work, where consciousness seems to build up slowly. The master will not recognize him as a self because he treats the servant like a tool, but the servant can recognize himself in what he makes, both in the physical object and in the intangible change it makes in him. In Hegel's words, the worker "comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence" (§195).

    The master is the easiest place to see what the servant gains. The master has "interposed the servant between the thing and himself," keeping only "the pure enjoyment of it" (§190). Pure here means nothing is mixed in, just consumption. So, Hegel says, his satisfaction is "only a fleeting one, for it lacks objectivity and permanence" (§195). The thing gets used up and the master has to want again. The servant's work goes the other way. It "requires desire held in check" and "forms and shapes the thing" (§195), and what the servant makes stays. This gap between wanting something and acting on it is where deliberation comes in. To shape a table, the servant has to picture what it could be before it exists, and that picturing takes time.

    His biggest decision takes time too. Every day he faces the same choice to keep working for the master or to fight him to be recognized. Fighting is never the option he chooses, but it is always there. That is what makes this deliberation, since the same two outcomes come back each day to be weighed again. The fear doesn't leave either, since his dread is not felt "merely at odd moments" (§194). His life gets staked slowly, through service, and each day he chooses to live, he gives up making the master see him as a self.

    That fear might start the servant's self-recognition, but Hegel doesn't think it can finish it. "Without the formative activity," he writes, "fear remains inward and mute" (§196). Mute is a strong word for it. Fear that never reaches the world can't change anything, and work is what gives it somewhere to go. Stoicism is a good test of this. The Stoic decides that his circumstances don't define him, which Hegel treats as a real but incomplete freedom. But the Stoic is the same "whether on the throne or in chains" (§199), and if each outcome is equal to him, what is left to weigh? He is still thinking. I'm just not sure he is deliberating anymore.

    Fanon describes what happens when someone else does the deliberating. "One day the White Master, without conflict, recognized the Negro slave" (217), and the formerly enslaved person "was acted upon" (220). The master weighed the options and made the decision, so the freedom that came after was freedom from slavery and not the kind Hegel ties to self-consciousness. The second kind cannot be handed over, because it depends on being recognized by someone of equal standing. Fanon still calls this freedom, but here it might be better described as self-consciousness itself. Even after the law freed him, the master still decided whether to see him as an equal, and recognition from one side only is not real recognition. A table cannot recognize the servant back, and neither can a master who recognizes him only because he chose to. Hegel's servant at least gets to weigh his choice every day. The formerly enslaved person got the outcome without any of that, which is why he "went from one way of life to another, but not from one life to another" (220).

    For Hegel and Fanon, then, consciousness seems earned through deliberation, not handed over. But Hegel complicates this. He writes that servitude "will ultimately turn into the opposite of what it immediately is" (§193), as if the servant's independence were already decided. If it is, is the servant really deliberating, or just following a path someone else laid out? And if his daily choices only get him halfway, what gets him the rest of the way? Is that last step even in his control, and if it isn't, does any of it count as deliberation?

    Build 4

    Individual · An objection to deliberation, and a reply

    In Build 3, I brought up the argument that Hegel and Fanon seem to make, that consciousness is earned through deliberation as opposed to just being handed over.

    Read Build 4

    In Build 3, I brought up the argument that Hegel and Fanon seem to make, that consciousness is earned through deliberation as opposed to just being handed over. The servant faces the same choice every day, keep serving the master or revolt against him. But one might object that deliberation does not fit Hegel's conception of consciousness, because the servant doesn't really make a choice at all. Nobody chooses to be scared of death. It just happens to them. In other words, the servant's whole being "has been seized with dread" by "the fear of death—the absolute Master" (§194). Seized means they get grabbed against their will and don't get a say, so there isn't really anything to deliberate. Hegel even writes that servitude "will ultimately turn into the opposite of what it immediately is" (§193), and ultimately makes the whole thing sound doomed to flip sooner or later anyway. Fanon's freed slaves have it the same way. The master recognized them "without conflict" (217), and without conflict means they never fought for it or chose anything. Colonialism "forces the people it dominates to ask themselves the question constantly," and that question is "In reality, who am I?" (250). The colonized person never chooses to ask it. Others who deny their humanity force it on them. If consciousness for both authors comes from being seized, forced, and fated, then deliberation looks like the wrong word for it.

    Where does the objection go too far? It is correct that fear is the servant's first moment and is not chosen, but the objection wrongly assumes fear is the end of the process. "Without the formative activity," Hegel writes, "fear remains inward and mute" (§196), and mute means the fear can do nothing until work gives it a way out. That work "requires desire held in check" (§195) is the part the objection misses, since holding something in check is already a decision. The servant did not choose servitude, but deliberation does not require that. For the master's order determines what is to be made by the servant, but not how it is to be made. Thus, each time work "forms and shapes the thing" (§195), the servant makes choices between different versions of it, and shapes leaves room for many versions. This is the deliberation referred to above.

    That still leaves "will ultimately," which seems to set a predetermined path. However, this is not what Hegel describes. He lists fear, service, and formative activity as all being "necessary" (§196), and necessary only says the stages must happen, not how. Each of these stages must be completed by the servant in their own way, and nothing in Hegel's account implies that the shaping happens of itself.

    Fanon sharpens the distinction between the forced and the freely chosen. "Man's behavior is not only reactional," he writes, and only admits that some of it still is. It is he who, "having taken thought, prepares to act" (222), that Fanon gives the real task to. Thus, having taken thought creates a space between being acted upon and acting, which is how desire is held in check. Although colonialism determined that the colonized would be asked who they are, it did not in fact determine how that question would be answered. Fanon refuses to let history answer for him, since "the past can in no way guide me in the present moment" (225), and in no way leaves the past no say. Rather, "I am endlessly creating myself" (229), and endlessly means the break with the past gets weighed again and again.

    Thus the objection deepens the claim instead of overturning it. At first sight, deliberation appears to be the weighing of scenarios that "do not automatically ensue as in physical causation," and automatically names the servant who only reacts. But as was noted, this deliberation does not take place within the freedom of a mind choosing between options. It begins when someone else forces something on the self, a master's threat or a colonizer's question, and the self holds that pressure in check long enough to turn it into options. Reacting is what fear does on its own. Deliberating is what consciousness does with fear. However, if one considers how Hegel and Fanon use deliberation in order to turn another's pressure into one's own options, one can begin to see how deliberation might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course. That leaves a question for the next Builds. If deliberation needs pressure from another to begin, can a self that was never pushed, like Hegel's master, deliberate at all?
