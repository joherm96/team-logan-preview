## Build 1
due: Sep 11
about: Individual · Hegel, *Phenomenology of Spirit* §181
passage-label: Passage · Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §181
passage: “This double-edged sense of supersession of its double-edged otherness is likewise a double-edged return into itself. For first, through the supersession, it receives back its own self, because, by superseding its otherness, it again becomes selfsame. However, second, it likewise gives the other self-consciousness back to itself, for it saw itself in the other, but it supersedes this being of itself in the other and thus sets the other free again.”

What Hegel presents here is a description of an ideal interaction between two self-consciousnesses. One self-consciousness meets another and sees itself reflected in the other. It seeks recognition by the other, but due to this “double-edged otherness,” receiving recognition also means giving it to the other, and in turn, setting the other free back into itself. It is a kind of social interaction, but one that is strictly bilateral, meaning both parties walk away having gained and given something. However, Hegel follows this section by introducing an important distinction: a consciousness can exist but without formal recognition it is not “actual,” and remains merely implicit. To become “actual” means there is certainty, and only through being tested and at risk can there be tangible evidence of an “earned” struggle. In essence, a consciousness can have experience and be introspective but it is only through recognition by another that it can be self-certain.

There is typically a shift in an exchange between two consciousnesses, one that creates a hierarchy where no actual mutuality is fostered, and only one self-consciousness can really become “for itself,” or be recognized as an “I.” This traditional hierarchy involves a person of superiority and their subordinate — but the two positions are not assigned by any external force; they are produced by the confrontation itself. If actuality requires a life-or-death circumstance and one party is willing to risk their existence while the other submits to preserve their own life, then it is impossible for the two to emerge as equals. Hegel believes the only sufficient evidence comes through risk; without it, there is no proof that recognition is “deserved.” The act of yielding to the other, then, is itself testimony of valuing physical survival over freedom. The inequality that follows is the direct result of a submission by one party, not an external factor. This is where his master-slave dialectic is applied.

In Hegel’s philosophy, the superseding between consciousnesses, as the passage itself mentions several times, is a translation of the German word Aufhebung, and is famously considered a double-edged sword. This is because the entire process involves simultaneously destroying, preserving, and elevating ideas. In the case of Hegel’s master-slave dialectic (lordship and bondage), this concept makes it clear this is a distorted version of the “ideal,” and not an entirely separate idea.

To move the system forward, the hierarchy is essentially abandoned, or destroyed. However, the effects on the master and slave do not land symmetrically; each preserves something different through the collapse. The slave is transformed by undergoing physical labor, in which fear and discipline were instilled. While the position as “slave” is negated, that process — acting on the real world, shaping an external object — helped him understand he was independent, with a self-consciousness. For the master, even though his position as a superior is also negated, nothing of value is preserved. He has experienced no labor, no discipline under fear, and has not developed skills to apply to the external world. The master has become completely dependent on the structure of the hierarchy, having consumed what is produced without ever doing the work. As a result, when the slave is moving through Aufhebung, the instant the structure disappears, the master’s lack of independence leaves no room for forward growth. There is no opportunity for recognition of the master, leaving him trapped while the slave unlocks the third step of this process: elevation. An elevation to self-consciousness. One that was earned.

Nevertheless, this raises a question that this passage doesn’t settle: is the bondsman’s self-consciousness actual and complete if it was created through labor on the external world but lacks formal recognition by the master? Or does the labor only bring him partway, still short of full actuality without the master’s recognition?

## Build 3

about: Individual · Hegel and Fanon on phenomenality

Initially, I understood Hegel’s terms of achieving self-consciousness through his “double-edged sense of supersession,” (§181) where two consciousnesses see themselves in one another and can therefore set each other free. This is the self-other relationship Hegel bases his account on: a consciousness develops into a self through an other it simultaneously depends on and distinguishes itself from. I also understood how the master-servant dialectic emerges from a life-and-death struggle, which Fanon later reframes. However, after several revisions, I now see that the relationship they both claim is necessary for the development of self-consciousness is unstable, because the self depends on an other it cannot control. Hegel presents it as a struggle two consciousnesses undergo, while Fanon argues that Black subjects are not just denied recognition but are never offered the struggle as an option to begin with. I had mistaken something structurally withheld from the start for an experience that simply lands unequally every time. Seeing this changes how I understand phenomenality, the idea that there is a “what it feels like to be me” that does not depend on recognition. But if recognition can be withheld entirely, then the “me” cannot be the stable starting point for consciousness that phenomenality assumes, since, as Fanon suggests, outside forces can shape it before a self can even identify it.

Hegel distinguishes the “I am I” (§167), the least developed form of consciousness, from the process of mutual supersession as the means to develop it into actual, self-certain consciousness. Phenomenality (“what it feels like to be me”) is an individualized experience and resembles the “I am I”; both are a “mere immediate consciousness.” (§189) Both are described without any relation to an other, which is why Hegel would disqualify each as an actual self-consciousness. Phenomenality, however, assumes a self is already capable of introspection and understanding the feeling of, for example, “seeing red.” This is the tension. Hegel says consciousness before recognition is not yet a self, while phenomenality treats that same consciousness as already a self.

Hegel asserts, “Each [self-consciousness] is indeed certain of its own self, but not of the other, and therefore its own self-certainty still has no truth.” (§186) In other words, a feeling can be certain of itself, but that certainty isn’t worth anything until tested against an other. This is where the self-other relationship becomes the center of the problem: if our sense of “me” exists only by distinguishing ourselves from an other, then it is inherently fragile because it depends on a relation that is not fixed. We would like to define ourselves with certainty, but this suggests we can’t do it alone. Fanon explores that same fragility, but asks what happens when something external does the defining.

Fanon’s application of Hegel brings us back to the moment before consciousnesses can even interact. Phenomenality assumes a stable concept of “what it feels like to be me,” while Fanon argues that for a Black individual, that definition is largely pre-determined by external factors, namely racism. Fanon says, “the Black man was acted upon,” (220) pointing to how colonialism imposed an identity on Black individuals. The line’s passive structure mirrors its claim: grammatically, the Black man is not acting, just as he is denied the agency to define himself. This is what it means for recognition to be withheld rather than unequal: he is not a participant who loses, he was never allowed into it. He does not get to decide his identity for himself, a societal consensus does that for him. Here is the instability Hegel describes at its extreme: the self depends on an other, but in this case that other designed the terms. But if, as Fanon suggests, a felt-experience can be altered or even authored by social or historical factors, is there a way to find an identity that is authentically one’s own? If not, true self-consciousness would only be available to those who implemented the social constraints in the first place.

Hegel traces the journey to self-consciousness; Fanon shows what happens when identity is shaped by the outside. I now read the instability of the self-other dynamic as the reason the “me” cannot be a reliable starting point, as it depends on an other it cannot control. Fanon later discourages dwelling on the past when he writes, “In the world through which I travel, I am endlessly creating myself.” (229) I view this as an opportunity, as a means of moving forward and continuously adapting himself despite the initial version of identity that was shaped against his will. The self can’t be a reliable starting point, as phenomenality assumes, but something that can constantly create itself against an other it cannot control. Whether this ever reaches Hegel’s full self-certainty remains open, but it is more than phenomenality can account for.

about: Individual · An objection to the unstable self, and a reply

In Build 3, I claimed that the “me” in “what it feels like to be me” (the self that owns the feeling, not the feeling itself) is unstable, because it exists only in relation to an other it cannot control. One might object that Hegel’s master-servant dialectic undermines this claim, and that phenomenality might fit Hegel’s picture better than I initially allowed. The servant, “forced back into itself” (§193), through labor performed under fear, will “transform into a truly independent consciousness” (§193). He gains what I read as a felt, first-person sense of his own independence, the felt “me,” without the master’s recognition. Hegel’s word is “see,” not “feel,” so I am not claiming he describes phenomenality; I am using it as a lens to ask whether this first-person “me” can hold itself up without anyone else. The lens fits because Hegel defines the servant’s discovery as a “rediscovery of himself by himself” (§196), a consciousness’ relation to itself, not a view from outside. On this reading, the self can build its own stability out of its work on the external world, and dependence on an other is not as problematic as I understood it.

Yet the text opposes a simple reading of the servant as self-sufficient because the felt “me” he gains is produced inside a relation he is supposed to escape. Hegel says fear, service, and formative activity are all necessary, and without the discipline of service, fear “remains mired in formality” (§196). Fear alone, then, does not make the felt “me”: the work gives him something to see himself in. In §195, Hegel writes that the servant “comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence.” His sense of “me” is found in what he made, not inside him, and what he made largely exists because he serves. His independence is therefore real as self-certainty, but it still depends on the master, who gives no recognition yet supplies the conditions—the fear and labor—under which the felt “me” is made.

Hegel’s next steps show why the servant’s independence cannot hold itself up alone. His labor gives him a felt certainty of himself, but not recognition, since the master still treats him as dependent. He tries to make that certainty enough by withdrawing into thought (§197), but in the stages that follow, what had been a relation between two people is “now lodged in one” (§206), leaving a self that holds both the master’s role and the servant’s. The dependence on an other does not end with the withdrawal; it moves inside the self. This is where I read phenomenality as fitting Hegel’s picture: the felt “me” is real, but when it tries to stand on its own, it reveals itself as divided between the two roles.

Fanon tests how far self-authored independence can go. His line “In the world through which I travel, I am endlessly creating myself” (229) works like the servant’s labor: authoring oneself does not rely on anyone’s recognition to happen. But “the black man was acted upon” (220) shows why that is not enough on its own. The passive sentence construction mirrors conditions that arrive before the self can respond, so even the felt “me” is not protected. More importantly, those conditions also define him for everyone else, whose recognition he would need. The self can therefore be largely self-determined and still unrecognized, which is the same gap the servant’s labor leaves open, only wider.

I previously claimed that the self is unstable because it depends on an other it cannot control. The objection shows that activity can build a real certainty, so “unstable” was too strong. I now claim that the felt “me” builds its stability inside relations it does not control, in two ways: certainty through its own activity, and recognition from others, which is never guaranteed. This changes how I read phenomenality: it is a real yet incomplete foundation. I say incomplete because a self that stands alone must hold both roles itself (§206), and vulnerable because, as Fanon shows, outside conditions can shape it before the self can respond.

However, if one considers how Hegel uses the servant’s labor in order to show a self that sees its own independence in what it makes, one can begin to see how phenomenality might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course, since in Hegel’s account the servant’s transformation cannot be explained without his seeing himself in the work. That intervention, though, cannot secure its own recognition, and, as Fanon adds, whether it counts depends on the others who receive it.

about: Individual · Hegel, Fanon and Scarry on phenomenality

Both Hegel and Scarry treat phenomenality, the first-person sense of what it is like to be oneself and what I call the felt “me,” as something that does not stay inside the self: it leaves through work and comes back changed. In Hegel’s master-servant dialectic, the servant, after fear and service, works on an object and “comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence” (§195), and is therefore transformed into an independent consciousness (§193). Creation, Scarry writes, is undertaken to “alter the felt-experience of sentience” (283), and the made object returns that force to its makers and “remakes” them (307). For both, the felt “me” is not an innate given, but is rather projected into something outside itself, and what returns changes it.

The two authors part ways over what completes the return. For Hegel, the object gives the servant a felt certainty of himself, but confirmation of the felt “me” has to be given by another consciousness, as in his ideal scenario, where each self “gives the other self-consciousness back to itself” (§181). The servant shows what happens when the other does not: he can see himself in the work he has made, but it was still made under and for a master who does not see him as an equal, so the recognition is one-sided. The work shows him what he can do, not that he counts, so the return gives his felt “me” certainty, not confirmation. Fanon takes this further. His subject is not resigned, as I initially understood him to be. He “wants a conflict,” because even hostility would give him something to answer. Most often, though, he meets “nothing but indifference, or a paternalistic curiosity” (221), so he can never be sure the white man considers him “consciousness in-itself-for-itself” (222). The servant at least entered Hegel’s struggle; Fanon’s subject is never allowed in.

Scarry locates what completes the return in the object. For her, the return does not wait on anyone acting; it is stored in the object by other people. An artifact cannot feel or recognize discomfort, yet its design “is the structure of a perception” (289). Her aspirin bottle carries “the projected knowingness of its collective makers” (305): its cap, label, and dose hold the knowledge that someone will want relief. The object returns relief and added power, easing “the aversiveness of sentience” while widening what sentience can do (305), but, as I read it, offers no confirmation that the felt “me” counts. Hegel’s return depends on someone’s act in the moment, while Scarry’s depends on someone’s anticipation in advance.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that the felt “me” is built through a loop that runs through something outside itself, a separate consciousness or a made object, and returns to change it. In Hegel and Fanon, that return arrives live, or fails to, when one consciousness gives the other back to itself (§181). In Scarry, it arrives stored in the bottle’s design. This pushes beyond my starting definition of phenomenality, an experience irreducible to neural activity alone: for both authors, felt experience is not contained in the person who has it and is completed outside them.

Hegel, Fanon, and Scarry all use phenomenality in order to show, as I argued in Build 4, that the felt “me” is real but cannot stand on its own. It is built through the self’s own work on the external world, but what that work returns depends on relations outside the self’s control. Yet, how can phenomenality allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? Essentially, phenomenality lets consciousness intervene by being a part of the loop that turns physical effort into lasting change. Felt pressure, like fear or pain, leads to action on the world, the action leaves something tangible, and what comes back remakes the person. Hegel’s servant discovers a felt sense of himself through his work, which is what converts labor from forced effort into self-change. In Scarry, the desire to be free of pain shapes made things like the bottle (305), which answers what pain is like, not just how the body works (283). The servant’s work leaves an object he can see himself in, while Fanon’s subject, who seeks a fight, meets indifference (221), so effort alone does not settle the outcome, as it depends on what is returned. This implies only a limited autonomy from the brain: even if felt states are realized in it, no account of one person alone can explain why the outcomes differ.
